## 1. Purpose

Framing Creativity Lab operates as an independent, founder-led research and development environment.

Its governance model defines how the Lab is directed, how responsibilities are assigned, how decisions are made, and how projects are managed from initial exploration to publication or retirement.

This document establishes a clear framework for:

- Maintaining the Lab's identity, mission, and strategic direction.
- Defining decision-making authority and responsibilities.
- Establishing expectations for contributors and collaborators.
- Managing projects, experiments, and public releases.
- Protecting intellectual property and confidential information.
- Handling disagreements, conflicts of interest, and governance concerns.
- Supporting the Lab's potential evolution over time.

The objective is to maintain a structure that is clear and accountable without introducing unnecessary administrative complexity.

Governance should support experimentation, not become an obstacle to it.

## 2. Governance Principles

The Lab's governance is based on the following principles.

### 2.1 Independence

Framing Creativity Lab is an independent initiative.

Its research direction, publication decisions, and internal operating principles are maintained independently unless a specific collaboration or agreement explicitly establishes otherwise.

Participation in the Lab does not imply affiliation with an external organization, company, institution, or commercial project.

### 2.2 Founder-Led Responsibility

The Lab is currently directed and maintained by its founder, J-ART.

The founder is responsible for the overall direction, standards, public identity, and operational integrity of the Lab.

Decision-making authority remains clearly assigned rather than being informally distributed or assumed.

### 2.3 Intellectual Freedom

The Lab encourages experimentation across artificial intelligence, creative technology, software engineering, digital experiences, and related disciplines.

Research proposals and creative approaches should be evaluated according to their relevance, quality, feasibility, and alignment with the Lab's principles rather than personal preferences or disciplinary boundaries alone.

### 2.4 Accountability

Every decision involving publication, access, intellectual property, or the use of external contributions should have an identifiable responsible party.

The Lab should communicate material decisions clearly and maintain appropriate records.

### 2.5 Proportionality

Governance requirements should reflect the scale, maturity, and risk of each project.

A small exploratory experiment should not require the same administrative process as a publicly deployed application handling sensitive information.

### 2.6 Transparency

The Lab aims to make its public scope, policies, project status, and contribution expectations understandable.

Transparency does not override confidentiality, privacy, security, or intellectual property obligations.

### 2.7 Responsible Evolution

The governance model may evolve as the Lab grows, welcomes more contributors, or develops new forms of collaboration.

Any such evolution should be intentional and documented.

## 3. Organizational Structure

### 3.1 Current Governance Model

Framing Creativity Lab currently operates under a founder-led governance structure.

The organizational model consists of:

| Role | Current responsibility |
|---|---|
| Founder and Maintainer | J-ART |
| Project Contributors | Individuals submitting accepted contributions |
| Collaborators | Individuals or organizations participating in specific agreed activities |
| External Providers | Third-party services, platforms, tools, and infrastructure providers |

This structure reflects the current operating model. It does not imply the existence of employees, departments, a board of directors, or a formal committee.

### 3.2 Founder and Maintainer

The founder and maintainer holds overall responsibility for the Lab.

Responsibilities include:

- Defining and maintaining the Lab's mission and strategic direction.
- Approving the creation and closure of Lab projects.
- Establishing publication and development standards.
- Reviewing project proposals and significant contributions.
- Approving public releases.
- Managing repository administration and access.
- Maintaining the Lab's policies and documentation.
- Addressing security, confidentiality, and governance concerns.
- Representing the Lab in official communications.
- Determining priorities and allocating available resources.

The maintainer may delegate specific tasks or responsibilities where appropriate.

Delegation does not automatically transfer overall governance authority or ownership of the Lab.

### 3.3 Contributors

Contributors participate by proposing, developing, reviewing, documenting, or improving Lab projects.

Depending on the nature of their participation, contributors may:

- Submit research ideas or technical proposals.
- Develop experimental features.
- Submit code or documentation.
- Review implementation approaches.
- Report bugs or security issues.
- Provide feedback on published work.
- Participate in agreed research or creative activities.

Contribution does not automatically grant repository administration rights, decision-making authority, ownership of the Lab, or permission to represent it externally.

### 3.4 Collaborators

Collaborators participate in specific activities or projects under an agreed scope.

Collaboration may involve:

- Technical development.
- Research activities.
- Creative experimentation.
- Design and prototyping.
- Knowledge exchange.
- Testing and evaluation.
- Documentation and publication.

The scope, responsibilities, attribution, confidentiality requirements, and intellectual property arrangements of a collaboration should be clarified before substantive work begins.

A collaboration does not automatically establish a partnership, employment relationship, agency relationship, or joint ownership arrangement.

### 3.5 External Providers

The Lab may rely on third-party platforms, models, APIs, libraries, infrastructure, and services.

External providers remain responsible for the services they operate, subject to their applicable terms and agreements.

The Lab should evaluate the suitability, security, licensing, and operational implications of external dependencies before adopting them.

Use of a third-party service does not imply endorsement, partnership, or formal affiliation.

## 4. Decision-Making Authority

Decision-making authority is assigned according to the nature and impact of each decision.

### 4.1 Strategic Decisions

Strategic decisions concern the overall identity, mission, priorities, and long-term direction of the Lab.

Examples include:

- Changes to the Lab's mission or objectives.
- Changes to its research domains.
- Establishment of new strategic initiatives.
- Significant changes to the public identity.
- Adoption of new governance structures.
- Decisions affecting the Lab's independence.

**Decision authority:** Founder and Maintainer.

### 4.2 Operational Decisions

Operational decisions concern the day-to-day management of the Lab.

Examples include:

- Repository administration.
- Project organization.
- Development workflows.
- Documentation standards.
- Access permissions.
- Tool selection.
- Internal priorities.

**Decision authority:** Founder and Maintainer, with delegated responsibilities where explicitly agreed.

### 4.3 Project Decisions

Project decisions concern the direction and implementation of individual projects.

Examples include:

- Accepting or rejecting a project proposal.
- Defining project scope.
- Selecting technical approaches.
- Establishing project milestones.
- Approving major architectural changes.
- Determining project maturity.
- Pausing or discontinuing an experiment.

**Decision authority:** Founder and Maintainer, with input from relevant contributors and collaborators.

Project-specific responsibilities may be delegated where appropriate, but the delegation must be explicit.

### 4.4 Publication Decisions

Publication decisions determine whether a project, release, document, or asset may become publicly accessible.

Examples include:

- Publishing source code.
- Releasing a prototype.
- Publishing research findings.
- Distributing generated or contributed assets.
- Creating public demonstrations.
- Publishing technical documentation.

**Decision authority:** Founder and Maintainer.

Publication must comply with the Publication Policy, applicable licenses, security requirements, confidentiality obligations, and relevant legal restrictions.

No contributor may assume that a submission or completed project is automatically approved for public release.

### 4.5 Security and Confidentiality Decisions

Security decisions may concern:

- Vulnerability handling.
- Credential exposure.
- Repository access.
- Confidentiality incidents.
- Data protection.
- Project withdrawal.
- Temporary suspension of a deployment or service.

**Decision authority:** Founder and Maintainer, subject to applicable legal obligations and relevant third-party procedures.

Urgent protective action may be taken before a complete review where reasonably necessary to reduce risk.

## 5. Decision-Making Process

The Lab uses a proportionate decision-making process.

Not every decision requires formal documentation or extensive review.

However, decisions with material consequences should have a clear rationale and responsible decision-maker.

### 5.1 General Process

Depending on the decision, the following process may be used:

1. **Identify:** Define the decision or issue.
2. **Understand:** Gather relevant technical, operational, creative, and legal context.
3. **Evaluate:** Consider alternatives, risks, resources, and consequences.
4. **Consult:** Seek input from relevant contributors or collaborators where useful.
5. **Decide:** Confirm the selected approach and the responsible decision-maker.
6. **Document:** Record material decisions when appropriate.
7. **Review:** Reassess the decision if new evidence or circumstances emerge.

### 5.2 Consultation

Consultation is encouraged when a decision affects contributors, collaborators, or project direction.

Consultation provides an opportunity to contribute expertise and identify potential consequences.

Unless explicitly agreed otherwise, consultation is advisory and does not constitute a transfer of decision-making authority.

### 5.3 Decision Criteria

Decisions should consider, where relevant:

- Alignment with the Lab's mission.
- Research and creative value.
- Technical feasibility.
- Quality and maintainability.
- Security and privacy.
- Intellectual property and licensing.
- Resource requirements.
- Operational complexity.
- Ethical and societal implications.
- Long-term sustainability.

The relative importance of these criteria depends on the nature of the decision.

### 5.4 Documentation

Material decisions may be recorded through:

- GitHub issues or discussions.
- Pull request reviews.
- Project documentation.
- Architecture Decision Records (ADRs).
- Research reports.
- Release notes.
- Policy revisions.

Sensitive or confidential decisions should not be documented in publicly accessible locations.

## 6. Project Governance

Each Lab project should have an appropriate level of structure based on its purpose, complexity, and maturity.

### 6.1 Project Initiation

A new project or experiment may begin through:

- A research question.
- A technical exploration.
- A creative concept.
- A prototype proposal.
- A documented problem or opportunity.
- An independently developed idea aligned with the Lab's mission.

Before significant development or public release, the project should have a sufficiently clear purpose and scope.

### 6.2 Project Definition

Depending on the project, the following information should be documented:

- Project name.
- Purpose and objectives.
- Research or technical question.
- Scope and boundaries.
- Expected outputs.
- Relevant dependencies.
- Technical approach.
- Known risks and limitations.
- Data and privacy considerations.
- Intellectual property considerations.
- Publication status.
- Responsible maintainer or project lead.

A small experiment may use a simplified project record.

More complex projects should have more detailed documentation.

### 6.3 Project Lifecycle

Projects may progress through the following stages:

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| Proposal | Initial idea, research question, or opportunity |
| Exploration | Preliminary investigation and feasibility assessment |
| Experimentation | Active testing, prototyping, and development |
| Validation | Evaluation of technical and research outcomes |
| Review | Assessment of quality, risks, and publication readiness |
| Publication | Approved release or public presentation |
| Maintenance | Ongoing updates, corrections, and support where applicable |
| Archived | Retained for reference but no longer actively maintained |
| Discontinued | Development stopped and the project formally closed |

Not every project must pass through every stage.

Some experiments may remain internal, be discontinued early, or be published as research notes rather than software releases.

### 6.4 Project Status

Project documentation should communicate the current maturity and maintenance expectations.

Where useful, the following classifications may be applied:

- **Concept:** An early-stage idea without a validated implementation.
- **Experimental:** A working exploration that may be incomplete or unstable.
- **Prototype:** A demonstrable implementation used to evaluate a concept.
- **Development:** An actively evolving project.
- **Validated:** A project that has passed its defined evaluation criteria.
- **Published:** A publicly released project or result.
- **Maintenance:** A project receiving ongoing corrections or improvements.
- **Archived:** A project retained without active development.
- **Discontinued:** A project no longer intended for further development.

These labels describe project maturity. They do not represent security certification, commercial readiness, or guaranteed support.

## 7. Repository and Access Governance

Repository permissions should reflect the responsibilities of each participant.

### 7.1 Access Principles

The Lab follows the principle of least privilege.

Users should receive only the access necessary to perform their agreed activities.

Repository permissions may include:

- Read access.
- Triage access.
- Write access.
- Maintain access.
- Administration access.

Not every contributor requires direct write access.

### 7.2 Access Approval

The maintainer approves access to official Lab repositories and associated resources.

Access may be granted according to:

- Project responsibilities.
- Contribution history.
- Required technical permissions.
- Security considerations.
- Collaboration agreements.

### 7.3 Access Review and Removal

Access may be reviewed or removed when:

- A collaboration ends.
- Responsibilities change.
- A participant no longer requires access.
- Security concerns arise.
- A policy violation occurs.
- A project is archived or discontinued.

Credentials and permissions should not remain active without a legitimate operational reason.

### 7.4 Administrative Control

Administrative privileges remain under the control of the maintainer unless explicitly delegated.

The transfer of administrative rights must be intentional and documented.

No contributor should assume that participation automatically includes administrative control over a repository.

## 8. Intellectual Property and Ownership

Intellectual property must be handled transparently and according to the applicable rights, licenses, and agreements.

### 8.1 Ownership of the Lab

The name, identity, governance, and overall direction of Framing Creativity Lab are maintained by its founder.

Participation in the Lab does not automatically provide ownership rights over the Lab, its identity, or its strategic decisions.

### 8.2 Ownership of Contributions

Submitting a contribution does not, by itself, transfer ownership of that contribution to the Lab or its maintainer.

Unless a separate agreement or applicable license establishes otherwise, contributors retain the rights they hold in their original work.

The permissions granted through a project's license or other applicable terms must be respected.

### 8.3 Third-Party Material

Contributors must not introduce third-party code, content, models, datasets, or assets without considering their licensing and usage restrictions.

Material with unclear ownership or incompatible licensing conditions should not be accepted for publication until the issue has been resolved.

### 8.4 Project-Specific Agreements

A project may require additional agreements covering:

- Joint development.
- Commercial exploitation.
- Attribution.
- Licensing.
- Confidential information.
- Data access.
- Distribution.
- Future maintenance.
- Use of contributed materials.

Such agreements must be established explicitly.

Silence or informal participation should not be interpreted as acceptance of ownership transfer or commercial rights.

### 8.5 Proprietary and Confidential Work

The public Lab repository must remain separate from private, proprietary, and confidential projects.

The existence of a related idea, technology, or research interest does not authorize the publication of private implementation details.

No contributor may use the Lab as a channel to disclose material from another project without appropriate authorization.

The Publication Policy governs the release of public material.

## 9. Collaboration Governance

Collaboration should be based on clear expectations and an agreed scope.

### 9.1 Before Collaboration Begins

For substantive collaborations, the parties should clarify:

- Purpose and expected outcome.
- Scope of work.
- Individual responsibilities.
- Communication channels.
- Contribution and attribution expectations.
- Intellectual property arrangements.
- Confidentiality requirements.
- Use of third-party tools and services.
- Publication and distribution permissions.
- Expected duration or completion criteria.

Not every informal exchange requires a written agreement.

However, significant commitments involving ownership, confidential material, financial obligations, or commercial use should be documented appropriately.

### 9.2 External Representation

Only individuals explicitly authorized by the maintainer may represent Framing Creativity Lab in an official capacity.

Contributors and collaborators must not imply that they speak on behalf of the Lab unless such authority has been granted.

They must not make unauthorized commitments, promises, partnerships, or public statements on its behalf.

### 9.3 Collaboration Boundaries

Collaboration does not automatically grant access to:

- Private repositories.
- Internal documentation.
- Confidential research.
- Personal or sensitive data.
- Proprietary assets.
- Administrative credentials.
- Unrelated projects or infrastructure.

Access must be explicitly authorized and limited to the agreed scope.

## 10. Conflicts of Interest

A conflict of interest may arise when personal, professional, financial, or organizational interests could influence a decision or create the appearance of compromised judgment.

Examples include:

- Evaluating a contribution involving one's own commercial interests.
- Selecting a supplier with whom the decision-maker has a financial relationship.
- Reviewing work where the reviewer has a direct competing interest.
- Using confidential Lab information for unrelated personal or commercial advantage.
- Making publication decisions that materially benefit an undisclosed external party.

Participants should disclose relevant conflicts when they may materially affect a decision.

The maintainer should assess whether the conflict requires:

- Additional disclosure.
- A change in decision-making responsibility.
- Independent input.
- Restricted access to sensitive information.
- Withdrawal from the relevant review or decision.

The existence of a conflict does not automatically imply misconduct.

The objective is to maintain informed and transparent decision-making.

## 11. Disagreements and Governance Disputes

Disagreements may arise concerning:

- Project direction.
- Technical architecture.
- Research methods.
- Creative interpretation.
- Publication decisions.
- Attribution and ownership.
- Contribution acceptance.
- Access privileges.
- Resource allocation.

The Lab encourages disagreements to be addressed through evidence, clear communication, and constructive discussion.

Where a disagreement cannot be resolved informally, the following approach may be used:

1. Clarify the subject and the decision being challenged.
2. Document relevant facts and concerns.
3. Allow affected parties to provide context.
4. Evaluate the applicable policies and project requirements.
5. Confirm the decision and its rationale.
6. Record material outcomes where appropriate.

The maintainer retains final decision-making authority under the current governance model, subject to applicable rights, agreements, and legal obligations.

This does not remove the obligation to act consistently with the Lab's Code of Conduct.

Concerns involving the maintainer should be handled with particular attention to conflicts of interest and the limitations of a founder-led review process.

## 12. Security, Privacy, and Risk Governance

Security and privacy considerations form part of project governance.

Projects should identify risks proportionate to their functionality and potential impact.

The maintainer is responsible for ensuring that relevant policies are considered before publication.

This includes:

- Credential management.
- Access control.
- Dependency review.
- Data minimization.
- Privacy considerations.
- Secure development practices.
- AI-specific security risks.
- Third-party service dependencies.
- Responsible vulnerability disclosure.

Where material risks cannot be reasonably addressed, a project may be restricted, delayed, modified, or withdrawn.

Relevant procedures are defined in `SECURITY.md` and `PUBLICATION-POLICY.md`.

## 13. Resource and Priority Management

Framing Creativity Lab is an independent initiative with finite time, technical capacity, and resources.

The maintainer determines which projects receive active attention.

Project prioritization may consider:

- Alignment with the Lab's mission.
- Research value.
- Creative potential.
- Technical feasibility.
- Available resources.
- External dependencies.
- Expected maintenance requirements.
- Security and operational risks.
- Long-term relevance.

A proposal may be accepted for exploration without a commitment to complete, publish, or maintain it.

Similarly, an existing project may be paused or discontinued when circumstances or priorities change.

Public project status should be updated when a material change affects expected maintenance or availability.

## 14. Governance Changes

The governance model may evolve as the Lab's scale and activities change.

Potential future developments may include:

- Delegated project maintainers.
- Additional repository administrators.
- Defined research or technical leads.
- External advisory participation.
- Project-specific governance arrangements.
- Formal collaboration agreements.
- Additional review or approval procedures.

These are possible future structures, not current organizational commitments.

Any change that materially modifies decision-making authority or responsibilities should be documented.

### 14.1 Delegation

The maintainer may delegate limited responsibilities to qualified individuals.

Delegation should define:

- The responsibility being assigned.
- The scope of authority.
- Relevant limitations.
- Duration or review conditions.
- Reporting expectations.

Delegated responsibilities may be revised or withdrawn where appropriate.

### 14.2 Structural Changes

The introduction of new governance roles or bodies should be based on actual operational needs.

A formal role should have a defined purpose, scope, and level of authority.

The Lab should avoid creating nominal positions that imply responsibilities or independence that do not exist in practice.

## 15. Continuity and Succession

The Lab's continuity depends on the availability of its maintainer and the responsible management of its public resources.

Where possible, the maintainer should preserve:

- Clear repository documentation.
- Version-controlled policies.
- Recoverable project files.
- Secure access management.
- Documented dependencies.
- Appropriate records of important decisions.

If the maintainer becomes unavailable, project activity may be paused until an authorized successor or alternative arrangement is established.

No contributor automatically becomes the successor, owner, or administrator of the Lab because of contribution history or repository activity.

Any transfer of governance authority, administrative access, or ownership must be explicitly authorized and handled in accordance with applicable rights and agreements.

---

## 16. Governance Records

The Lab may maintain records of material governance decisions to support continuity, accountability, and institutional memory.

Relevant records may include:

- Policy versions and revision history.
- Project approval or discontinuation decisions.
- Major technical decisions.
- Delegation of responsibilities.
- Publication approvals.
- Significant collaboration agreements.
- Changes to repository permissions.
- Material security or confidentiality decisions.

Records should be proportionate to the nature of the decision.

Sensitive information should be stored in appropriate restricted locations rather than public repositories.

## 19. Closing Principle

Governance exists to provide direction, clarity, and continuity.

It should protect the conditions that allow independent research and creative experimentation to flourish, while ensuring that responsibility remains visible and decisions remain understandable.

A flexible structure does not mean an absence of rules. An independent initiative still requires clear boundaries, accountable decisions, and respect for the people and work involved.

**Independence requires responsibility. Creativity requires freedom. Good governance makes room for both.**
